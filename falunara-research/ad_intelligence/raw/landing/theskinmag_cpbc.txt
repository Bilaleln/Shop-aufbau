In 1973, a Biochemist Pulled Something Strange Out of Human Blood. This Discovery Explains Everything About Your Crepey Arms.


Dr. Loren Pickart wasn't your typical scientist.

While his colleagues chased cancer cures in the late 1960s, the Minnesota-born researcher chose to study human aging instead. As he later joked in an interview, "that way I would always have a job."

He earned his PhD in biochemistry at the University of California, San Francisco. He published research on one molecule for more than five decades.

He founded a biotech company around his discovery and took it public on the NASDAQ.

And before he passed away in 2023 at age 85, the peptide he found had quietly become a staple in dermatologists' offices, used around professional skin procedures, and a fixture in hair clinics for decades.

But it all started with something strange he kept noticing in his lab at UCSF.

Blood from young donors behaved differently than blood from older donors.

When tissue from older donors was exposed to plasma from young donors, the aging cells changed.

They began producing proteins more typical of young, healthy tissue. Something in young blood was carrying an instruction. An order to repair.

His answer to that mystery became his 1973 doctoral thesis, titled "A TriPeptide from Human Serum."

He had isolated one of the smallest signaling molecules ever found in human blood: just three amino acids bound to a single copper ion. 

Scientists call it GHK-Cu. On an ingredient label, it's Copper Tripeptide-1.

Copper Tripeptide-1 — three amino acids bound to a single copper ion. One of the smallest signalling molecules ever isolated from human blood.

The #1 Reason Crepey Skin Shows Up After 50 (Hint: It's Not Aging)

Most women think crepey skin is just aging. The research says it's not.

Dig through the published studies and you find something almost no one in skincare talks about: the cells responsible for your skin's firmness don't just slow down as you age. They stop getting their orders.

Here's what the research shows.

Fibroblasts are cells that live in your skin. You can think of them like the crew that builds collagen and elastin.

They're the reason skin stays thick, firm, and smooth. And they take their work orders from signaling molecules.

Chief among them: the copper peptide Dr. Pickart discovered.

In 2018, Pickart and a colleague published a paper in the International Journal of Molecular Sciences with a measurement that explains everything.

At age 20, your blood plasma carries roughly 200 nanograms per milliliter of GHK.

By age 60, it has fallen to about 80.
